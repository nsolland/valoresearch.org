A simple explanation

Imagine you ask someone to buy milk.

Sounds simple. But if you actually want to make sure the job is done correctly, “buy milk” isn’t enough.

First: Are you allowed to buy milk?

Then: What needs to happen to get it done? Where do you need to go? Who will you meet? Which people, systems, and services are involved along the way?

Then we need to know what “milk” actually means.

Whole or skimmed? Organic? Lactose-free? Which brand? What size? Is the definition precise enough to know whether you actually bought the right milk?

Can you afford it?

How will you get to the store? Is the route safe? Has anything changed since the task was given?

What do you know about the milk before you buy it?

Where did it come from? How was it produced, transported, and stored? Was the cold chain maintained? Could it have been contaminated or tampered with before it reached the shelf?

And before you buy it, you also need to know what happens afterwards.

How will it be transported home? How will it be stored? Who will have access to it? Can you protect it from tampering? Can you keep it at the right temperature? Can you preserve its integrity all the way until it is actually consumed?

It is not enough that someone bought the “right type of milk.” It must be the same physical carton that was bought, transported, stored, and finally consumed — without someone swapping it along the way.

If you cannot establish a safe path from the shelf to the person who will drink it, you should not buy it.

Buying the right milk is not enough if you cannot deliver the right milk safely.

Finally, you are standing at the checkout with one specific carton.

Now the question has to be asked again:

Is this exact action — with this exact milk, through this exact path, under the conditions that apply now — still correct and authorized?

If yes, the action can proceed.

If no, it stops.

If we do not know, it escalates.

And afterwards, you must be able to answer:

Which milk was it? Why was that one chosen? What path did it take? Which controls did it pass? Could anyone have tampered with it before, during, or after? And was it actually consumed by the person it was intended for — in the same condition?

That is what Consequence Governance is really about:

Making sure you get the right, good, and safe milk you asked for — and that you can verify it before you drink it.

The final rule is simple:

If you cannot verify that the milk can be bought, transported, delivered, and consumed safely under the conditions that apply now, do not buy it.

Do not ask someone else to buy it for you. Do not take shortcuts because you are craving milk. Do not substitute “any milk” just to complete the task.

If the governed path says no, it does not become yes just because you change the path, change the store, or ask someone else to do it.

No means no.
